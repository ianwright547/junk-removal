Hurricane Debris Cleanup in Central Florida: What the County Takes and What It Does Not

After a storm, county debris collection covers less than most people assume. Here is the split, and how to avoid waiting weeks for a pickup that was never coming.

After a named storm, Central Florida counties usually run a special debris collection. It is genuinely useful and it is also narrower than people expect, which is how a pile sits at a curb for three weeks before somebody realises it was never on the list.

The distinction that matters is between storm-generated debris and everything else.

What county storm collection generally covers

Post-storm passes are typically limited to debris the storm itself created, separated into categories:

  • Vegetative debris: limbs, branches, palm fronds, trunks
  • Construction and demolition debris from storm damage: fence sections, roofing, siding, drywall from flooding
  • Sometimes white goods: appliances ruined by flooding, handled separately because of refrigerant
  • Sometimes household hazardous waste, also separated

What it does not cover

This is where people get caught. Normal household junk that happened to be put out at the same time is not storm debris, and crews will drive past it.

Furniture from a garage that did not flood, an old mattress, construction debris from a renovation that predates the storm, and anything in bags or boxes generally does not qualify. Several counties explicitly exclude bagged material because crews use grapple trucks that cannot handle bags.

Separate your piles or lose the pickup

The single most common mistake is one mixed pile. Grapple trucks make separate passes for separate categories. A pile of branches with a sofa in the middle of it is not a vegetative pile any more, and it may get skipped entirely on every pass.

Keep vegetative separate from construction debris, keep appliances separate, keep hazardous separate, and keep it all out of the road and away from meters, hydrants and low lines.

How long it actually takes

First passes in hard-hit areas often run weeks after the storm, and a full sweep can take a month or more. If you can live with the pile, waiting is free and is genuinely the right call for a big vegetative pile.

Hiring a hauler makes sense for the things the county will not take, for anything that is a hazard or a mosquito breeding site where it sits, and for rentals and businesses that cannot wait.

Before the next storm

The cheapest debris cleanup is the one you avoid. Clearing a garage of what you were going to get rid of anyway means less to become debris, and it means the garage can hold the patio furniture when the next warning comes.

The short version

Separate your piles by category, keep bagged and boxed material out of them, and understand that anything not created by the storm is yours to handle. Check your own county's current guidance after every storm, because the rules and the categories change per event.
