Downsizing a Home: A Checklist That Respects What Matters

A realistic plan for moving from a long-held family home into something smaller, without the weekend that ends in an argument.

Downsizing after thirty or forty years in one house is not a decluttering project. It is a series of decisions about a life, compressed into a few weeks because a closing date says so.

The families who get through it well are not the ruthless ones. They are the ones who started earlier and made fewer decisions per day.

On this page

  1. Start with the new floor plan, not the old house
  2. Three months, not three weekends
  3. Photograph before you let go
  4. Let the family claim things properly
  5. Be realistic about resale value
  6. Do not move boxes you have not opened
  7. Book the clearing before you need it

Start with the new floor plan, not the old house

Measure the new place first. Know exactly how many rooms, how much closet space and how much storage there is.

This converts an emotional question into a practical one. The dining set does not fit is a fact. The dining set has to go is an argument.

Three months, not three weekends

Roughly a room a week is sustainable. Three days of solid sorting is not, and it is how people either give up or throw out things they later want back.

Start with the rooms that carry the least meaning: garage, linen closet, spare bedroom. Leave the personal rooms until last, when the decisions have become easier to make.

Photograph before you let go

Many attachments are to the memory rather than the object. A good photograph of the workbench, the Christmas decorations, the kids' height marks on the door frame keeps the memory without the cubic feet.

This one suggestion does more to unstick a downsizing than any other.

Let the family claim things properly

Have each person privately list a small number of items that matter to them, then compare lists.

The lists usually barely overlap, because people want different things for different reasons. It turns a general argument into two or three specific conversations.

Be realistic about resale value

  • Usually worth less than hoped: formal china, collectible plates, most furniture from the seventies and eighties, encyclopedias, most pianos.
  • Often worth more than assumed: mid-century furniture, hand tools, certain costume jewellery, vinyl records, cameras, militaria, quality cast iron.
  • If there is a lot of either category, an appraiser costs far less than what gets thrown away without one.

Do not move boxes you have not opened

Anything that has been sealed in a garage or attic unopened for years should be opened and sorted before it moves, not after.

Moving an unopened box to a smaller home just relocates the decision and consumes space you do not have.

Book the clearing before you need it

The last pass has to happen between the move and the closing, which is a narrow and busy window.

Book it as soon as you have the dates rather than in the week itself.

The short version

Measure the new place first, work a room a week over months rather than days, and photograph the things you cannot keep. The deadline is the only part you cannot control.
